Milton Babbitt (1916–2011) studied composition with Roger Sessions at Princeton University, where he joined the faculty in 1938 and taught for most of his career. During World War II he began extending Schoenberg's twelve-tone method beyond pitch to duration, dynamics, timbre, and register, arriving independently at what became known as [total serialism](/library/total-serialism/); his *Three Compositions for Piano* (1947) are among its earliest examples. In 1959 he co-founded the [Columbia-Princeton Electronic Music Center](/library/columbia-princeton-electronic-music-center/) with Otto Luening, Vladimir Ussachevsky, and Roger Sessions, drawn to the [RCA Mark II Sound Synthesizer](/library/rca-mark-ii-sound-synthesizer/)'s ability to realize precisely serialized rhythm beyond what live performers could execute. His electronic works composed there include *Composition for Synthesizer* (1961) and *Ensembles for Synthesizer* (1964), and *Philomel* (1964), for soprano with synthesized accompaniment and manipulated recorded voice. He received a Pulitzer Prize Special Citation in 1982 and a MacArthur Fellowship in 1986.
